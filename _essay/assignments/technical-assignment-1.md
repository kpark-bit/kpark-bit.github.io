### Their Arduous March: The Inter-Korean Border, Nonhuman Displacement, and the Translation of Crisis



   In the summer of 1975, reported cases of animal rabies reached their highest level since Korea’s liberation in 1945, throwing South Korea into crisis. Rabies control in Seoul soon shifted from vaccinating pet dogs to culling street dogs. As the city’s surging dog population outstripped the state’s supply of rabies vaccine, the authoritarian government turned to coercion. At roughly the same time, Park Chung Hee, who ruled South Korea from 1961 to 1979, refer to North Korea in declaring that “a mad dog needs a club.”{% include essay/feature/aside.html

The two anticommunist posters shown here further testify to the Cold War conditions that enabled this coercive turn in disease control. The dog-catching squads that moved through Seoul’s alleys protected citizens from disease; they also advertised, in everyday life, the efficacy of authoritarian rule in eliminating threats. In fact, the state did erase dogs from the streets and rabies receded.

   Yet this success rested on an assumption that the city was ecologically separate from the world beyond it. The challenge to that premise came from across the DMZ. During North Korea’s famine of the 1990s, its forests were rapidly stripped bare; degradation was particularly severe in North Hwanghae Province, across the border from northern Greater Seoul.{% include essay/feature/aside.html

By turning to that earlier struggle, the term recasts the crisis of the 1990s as another ordeal that could be overcome under the leadership of the Kim family. As their habitats disappeared, omnivorous raccoon dogs were driven south along waterways shared by the two Koreas.

In this way, rabies acquired a new vector and returned to the borderlands of northern Greater Seoul. The map shown here records the consequence. The concentration of outbreaks after 1993 along Greater Seoul’s northern border quietly attests to how North Korea’s economic crisis crossed the border through wild animals and was translated into a public health crisis in the South.
