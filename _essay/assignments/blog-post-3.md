#### Unflattening History


When the speakers described critical making as a way out of the “tyranny of the essay,” I hesitated. I am a graduate student in history, trained to think through writing and evaluated by my adviser through it. Reading sources, constructing narratives, and supporting arguments with footnotes have always seemed to me the substance of historical scholarship. However, the note made me see how few exits from prose my conception of history allowed. I began to wonder whether this idea, still widely shared within the discipline, might help explain why historians’ voices seem increasingly faint in our society.


History itself, however, hardly lacks an audience, at least in South Korea and the United States, the two societies I move between. It thrives on YouTube and podcasts, in popular books, entertainment shows, and films. What has changed is the speed and compression of historical narratives. An era or event can be condensed into minutes, sometimes seconds, and audiences use these compressed accounts to understand the world, form views of the past, and eventually choose political positions.


The real issue is what gets flattened these days. Historical accounts become stories centered on heroic individuals, linear causation, and narratives in the service of nationalism. Such simplified pasts are easily mobilized: clear-cut divisions between good and evil justify present conflicts and hatreds, while violence that does not fit heroic narratives is erased from accounts of both the past and the present. Academic history has the complexity to challenge these accounts. Yet that complexity often remains confined to difficult prose that rarely reaches wider audiences. Perhaps it is not history but the forms and language of academic history that people are turning away from.


The lecture by Johnson and Salter suggested another possibility. Agentic AI can now produce things in digital and even physical worlds with minimal human intervention. Yet the human role has not disappeared. The question is whether we direct the process or merely approve its results. They warned of the “reverse centaur,” with the machine as head and the human as body, reduced to approving AI-generated outputs. The alternative is the centaur: the human remains the head, directing the machine’s power with vision and intention. I wondered whether historians who understand how things are made and know when to intervene might make games, websites, sculptures, or even slogans on T-shirts into forms of historical scholarship. Games, for instance, could let players make choices and experience their consequences, conveying through experience rather than narration that the past was shaped by contingency and intersecting causes, not inevitability. In the lecture’s terms, this would be “unflattening” history—giving it depth.


There are, of course, reasons for caution. In relying on commercial platforms to make things, historians may escape the confines of the university only to find themselves behind corporate walls. Adobe’s discontinuation of Flash support, which left countless works inaccessible, reminds us that what we create may not endure. More fundamentally, making is an act of selection. In trying to give depth to some aspects of the past, we risk flattening others. Even so, I am unwilling to give up on making as a way of reaching the public. Staying within the university for fear of making mistakes may be less an act of prudence than of evasion. We can only acknowledge these limits and keep trying.
