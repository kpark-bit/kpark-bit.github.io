The Prusa SL1S resin printer was the device that I used to produce a model terrain of northern Greater Seoul, a central space of analysis of my ongoing project on wildlife rabies, where rivers originating in North Korea converge in the South. Pressed for time by other classes, I chose this printer because its reservation calendar was nearly empty. I had initially assumed the work would be finished once the printing was complete. However, I discovered that the model also required curing. That is when I understood why the machine was not preferred by others. Yet when I placed my model alongside my classmates’ filament prints, the advantages of resin became clear. It captured even the finest contours, and its smooth finish made the palm-sized model feel like a real landscape. At the same time, I realized what this smoothness that my model has concealed. The model bore no trace of the border dividing North and South Korea, or of the boundaries separating wildlife habitats from the spaces inhabited by people and livestock.


If mapping, as Gómez (2026) suggests, requires us to ask what it excludes, critical making must go beyond the precise reproduction of terrain to inscribe relationships that remain invisible on its surface. What I need to inscribe on my model of central Korea is the history of how people have transformed this landscape. In North Korea, deforestation has destroyed wildlife habitats; in South Korea, Greater Seoul has expanded toward the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ). These developments have disrupted ecosystems, raising concerns about zoonotic disease. If I could render these changes visible on the model, viewers would be able to experience firsthand how nature transformed by human intervention has, in turn, affected everyday life. Through this project, I began to consider how I might move beyond precise making and give material form to the relationships and processes buried beneath it.
