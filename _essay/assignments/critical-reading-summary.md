order: 2
part: Assignments

Chapter 9, “Generation,” of Anastasia Salter and Emily K. Johnson’s *Critical Making in the Age of AI* (2025) examines the challenges that generative AI poses for higher education. The advent of AI has exposed the limitations of conventional assessment practices that rely on finished assignments to gauge students’ understanding. Salter and Johnson see this disruption as an opportunity to rethink not only how universities assess learning but also what they expect students to learn. Specifically, they propose moving beyond the essay as the primary mode of assessment and embracing a broader range of critical making. I agree with their diagnosis: when a finished product can no longer reliably demonstrate what a student has learned, assessment practices must change. I am less persuaded, however, by the educational future they envision. The critical making they advocate still depends on the ability to write with precision. To instruct and supervise AI, evaluate its outputs, and provide meaningful feedback, students must first be able to reason independently and articulate their thoughts in writing. Calls to decenter the essay therefore risk unintentionally pushing writing instruction to the margins. What needs to change first is not the place of writing in higher education but the way writing itself is assessed—as a process rather than a product.


Generative AI can already produce writing at a level expected of undergraduates. Detection tools not only struggle to keep pace with advancing AI models but also introduce another layer of opacity. Does this spell a crisis for higher education? Salter and Johnson suggest otherwise. To put current anxieties into perspective, they recall the unease that accompanied the introduction of calculators and Wikipedia. Rather than relying on prohibition or detection, they urge educators to reconsider the learning intent of individual assignments. Their proposal draws on constructivist learning theory, which holds that students construct their own understanding through the act of making. What matters, then, is not so much the finished product as the process through which it takes shape. Critical making, in this framework, turns AI’s imperfections into material for critical inquiry. Students might, for instance, experiment with the language they use to interact with AI, exploring where meaning is conveyed or lost and what is missing from the data on which the model was trained. In doing so, they intervene critically in the making process rather than simply accepting what AI produces. Such exercises allow students to discover both how quickly AI can generate plausible work and how readily its limitations become apparent when confronted with logical reasoning or the specific requirements of an assignment.


One of the chapter’s contributions lies in turning AI’s limitations into resources for teaching. This approach rests on the authors’ understanding of AI training data as a kind of archive. Archives are not neutral repositories of information. What they preserve and what they leave out reflect decisions made by particular groups, often those in positions of power, according to their interests and priorities. To take archival materials at face value is therefore to risk reproducing the biases embedded in their selection. The same holds true for AI. Its outputs are shaped not only by distortions within the data itself but also by the backgrounds and biases of those who selected it and by what data were available in the first place. Salter and Johnson thus treat these biases and omissions, along with data collected without consent and the uncompensated labor of data labeling, not as flaws to be concealed but as problems students can discover and examine through their work with AI. The implications are considerable. Students learn not simply to use a tool effectively but to question the tool itself and the knowledge it produces, developing their critical faculties in the process. The authors further envision critical making as a means of looking “beyond the essay.” Students can use AI to create images, videos, three-dimensional objects, and computer code, asking new questions appropriate to each form of creation.


I am not convinced, however, that the essay is simply one mode of learning among many. It occupies a distinctive place precisely because writing an essay provides sustained training in the ability to think and express oneself through prose. Whatever form the finished work takes, much of the process of making with generative AI unfolds through written language. Students write instructions, critically assess the responses they receive, and revise their instructions in light of that assessment. This cycle of writing, examining, rewriting, and examining again closely resembles the process of composing an essay. Yet Salter and Johnson describe these exchanges as a “conversation” with AI or a “negotiation” with the model without directly addressing the writing skills on which successful critical making depends. Expanding into other forms of critical making can wait until students have developed those skills. We should begin instead by keeping the essay at the center of instruction while shifting its assessment from the finished product to the writing process. One approach would be to divide essay writing into smaller stages, asking students at each stage to explain orally how well their drafts convey what they originally intended to say and providing feedback accordingly. Students trained in this way would be better equipped to direct AI and critically evaluate its responses without surrendering control over the process.
