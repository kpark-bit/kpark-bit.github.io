part: Assignments

Anastasia Salter and Emily K. Johnson’s “Generation,” chapter 9 of *Critical Making in the Age of AI* (2025), examines the challenge generative AI poses to higher education. When AI can produce a polished assignment, the finished product becomes a less reliable measure of what a student has learned. The authors see an opportunity in this predicament. They urge universities to rethink both how they teach and what they expect students to learn, moving beyond the essay as the main form of assessment toward a broader practice of critical making. I agree that assessment centered on finished products can no longer serve its old purpose. I am less convinced, however, by the authors’ vision of what should replace it. Critical making with AI still demands considerable skill in writing. As people handle and evaluate the works of AI, the ability to reason and to articulate that reasoning in words has become important more than ever. To decenter the essay, then, risks pushing writing ability aside when it is needed the most. The more pressing task is to make writing itself a process-centered practice.


AI has already demonstrated its ability to produce undergraduate-level prose. Detection tools struggle to keep pace and, more troublingly, introduce another layer of opacity. Does this spell a crisis for higher education? Salter and Johnson think not. Recalling the unease that accompanied calculators and Wikipedia, they argue against bans and detection in favor of rethinking the “learning intent” of individual assignments. Their alternative draws on constructivist learning theory: students develop understanding by making things, and the process of making matters more than the finished artifact. Within this framework, AI’s shortcomings become material for critical inquiry. Students revise their instructions as though conversing with the model, testing what their words convey, what gets lost, and what its training data leave out. Most assignments discussed in the chapter culminate in evaluating these outputs and reflecting on their implications. Students discover not only how quickly AI can produce convincing work but also how readily it falters in logical reasoning or in meeting the specific demands of a task.


One of the chapter’s strongest contributions is its transformation of AI’s limitations into resources for learning. This is possible because the authors approach training data as an archive. Archives are not neutral repositories of everything that happened. What to preserve and what to exclude reflect the perspectives of particular groups, mostly those with power. To accept an archive uncritically is to risk reinforcing its biases. The same is true of AI. Its outputs reflect not only distortions within the data but also who selected the data, whose perspectives informed that selection, and what materials were available in the first place. Salter and Johnson ask students to examine these omissions and biases. These are conditions to uncover through working with AI. Students thereby learn to critically review both the tools they use and the work those tools produce. The authors also see critical making as a way to “look beyond the essay.” By using AI to make images, videos, and code, students can experiment with other forms of expression and ask new questions of their work.


Yet the essay is not simply one learning activity among many. Writing an essay trains students to think and express their intentions through language. Nor does making with AI necessarily take us beyond writing. Whatever form the final artifact takes, much of the process unfolds in words: students describe what they want, evaluate what AI returns, and write new instructions in response. This cycle of writing, reading, revising, and reading again resembles the work of composing an essay. Salter and Johnson call it a ‘conversation’ with AI or a ‘negotiation’ with the model, but they do not directly address the writing skills on which successful making depends. I would reverse their priorities. Expanding into other forms of critical making can wait until students have developed a firmer command of writing. Rather than removing the essay from the center of assessment, we should shift our attention from the finished essay to its composition. One possibility is to divide writing into smaller stages, using brief oral conferences at each stage to ask students how faithfully their drafts express what they intended and to offer feedback for revision. Students trained in this way would be better equipped to direct AI, judge its responses, and maintain their own intentions through successive exchanges.
