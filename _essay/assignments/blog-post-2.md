### Making a Collage: Reading Greater Seoul’s Urban Metabolism through HGIS and Remote Sensing

I study the environmental history of Greater Seoul since Korea’s liberation in 1945 through the lens of urban metabolism. Following the movement of materials and living organisms between the city and its hinterland brings me back to the same problem. The processes I trace rarely appear in full in any single kind of source. The informal network that carried Seoul’s leftovers to pig farms on the outskirts, for instance, comes into view only in fragments scattered across newspapers and swine industry journals. With civilian access to the border zone severely restricted, no ecological survey directly documented the southward movement of raccoon dogs across the Military Demarcation Line or the renewed spread of rabies in the 1990s. I have therefore built my arguments by piecing together traces from different sources and asking what they can plausibly explain together. So far, I have done that work in prose. I now want to use historical geographic information systems (HGIS) and remote sensing to assemble those fragments into a more precise collage.


HGIS brings scattered records into a common spatial framework, making it possible to examine how they relate to one another. In my research on urban pig-raising, changes in the geography of pig farming and the conditions of road transport help explain why the food-waste circulation network broke down so rapidly in the late 1980s. By then, pig farms that had been pushed to the urban fringe had already moved beyond Seoul’s administrative boundaries. As car ownership surged, the distances between collection sites in central Seoul remained short, but the trips took longer to complete. Eventually, the labor cost of collecting leftovers exceeded the cost of buying feed. By plotting the pig-farming villages and collection sites I identify in newspapers and trade journals on historical road networks and estimating travel times, I could trace which sites were likely to have dropped out first and estimate the point at which collection ceased to make economic sense. In my research on rabies control, HGIS would let me overlay rivers shared by North and South Korea, the locations of recorded animal rabies cases, and areas where oral vaccine baits were distributed. I could then analyze spatial relationships that I have so far had to infer by looking back and forth between separate maps.


If HGIS assembles existing fragments into a map, remote sensing can supply a new kind of fragment for that collage. I have argued that North Korea’s food and energy crises accelerated forest degradation, pushing raccoon dogs out of their habitats and triggering a zoonotic disease crisis in Greater Seoul. Much of my evidence comes from a handful of photographs taken on the ground in North Korea in the 2010s and province-level figures from a secondary report based on satellite imagery from 1998–99. Together, these sources show severe forest degradation in North Hwanghae Province, across the DMZ from Greater Seoul, and suggest that it was concentrated in the watersheds of the Imjin River and Samicheon, which served as corridors for raccoon dogs. The available evidence leaves open exactly when and to what extent the forests in those watersheds deteriorated. Without that chronology, it is difficult to establish how North Korea’s economic crisis and South Korea’s zoonotic disease crisis were related. What if I applied a distant viewing approach to the extensive archive of Landsat imagery of North Korea dating back to the 1970s? I could compare changes in forest cover by watershed before and after the North Korean famine of the 1990s and see how the timing of those changes aligned with the resurgence of rabies in South Korea.
