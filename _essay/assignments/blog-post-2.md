### Making a Collage: Reading Greater Seoul’s Urban Metabolism through HGIS and Remote Sensing


I study the environmental history of Greater Seoul since liberation in 1945 through the lens of urban metabolism. Tracing the flows of materials and living things between the city and its surrounding areas, I am repeatedly reminded of how rarely the subjects I pursue can be fully captured in any single type of historical source. For instance, the informal network that carried food waste from Seoul to pig farms on its outskirts emerges only in fragments from newspapers and swine industry journals. Likewise, because civilian access to the borderlands remains severely restricted, no ecological survey has directly documented the southward movement of raccoon dogs across the Military Demarcation Line in the 1990s or its connection to the resurgence of rabies. Therefore, my main arguments are built on the plausibility that emerges when scattered traces from disparate sources are pieced together into a coherent narrative. So far, prose has been my primary tool for this work. Now I am hoping to use two digital methods, historical GIS (HGIS) and remote sensing, to turn these loose connections into a more carefully and firmly assembled collage.


HGIS offers a way to bring scattered fragments of historical evidence into a shared spatial framework and reveal relationships among them. In my project on pigs, for example, a major reason for the rapid collapse of the food-waste circulation network in the late 1980s was the changing geography of the city and its transportation system. Pig farms pushed toward the urban periphery had by then moved beyond Seoul’s administrative boundaries. Meanwhile, the explosive growth in automobile traffic meant that collecting food waste from sites within the city took increasingly longer, even when the distances remained short. Eventually, the labor costs of collection exceeded the cost of feed itself. By plotting the pig-farming villages and collection sites I have identified in newspapers and trade journals onto historical road networks and estimating travel times, I could begin to trace which collection sites dropped out of circulation first and where the network reached the point at which it was no longer economically viable. HGIS could also help my research on rabies control by allowing me to overlay rivers shared by North and South Korea, recorded cases of animal rabies, and areas where vaccine baits were distributed. Spatial relationships that I currently have to infer by moving back and forth between separate maps could then become objects of direct analysis.


How I make these maps, however, matters as much as what I hope to accomplish with them. As McDonough (2024) argues, translating historical sources into spatial data is not simply a matter of extracting facts that already exist. Rather, it is an iterative process of asking questions and evaluating results, through which data and interpretation take shape together. Both my projects on pigs and rabies in Greater Seoul have already taken substantial shape in prose. Precisely because these projects are well underway, I want to resist the temptation to make maps fit conclusions I have already reached. Instead, I hope to remain open to the possibility that the process of mapping will generate new interpretations and, ultimately, require me to rewrite the prose itself.


If HGIS assembles existing fragments into a collage in the form of a map, remote sensing can produce new kinds of fragments to add to it. I have argued that North Korea’s food and energy crises accelerated forest degradation, driving raccoon dogs out of their habitats and precipitating a zoonotic disease crisis in Greater Seoul. Much of the evidence for this argument, however, comes from a handful of photographs taken in North Korea during the 2010s and province-level figures from a secondary report analyzing satellite imagery from 1998–99. These sources point to severe forest degradation in North Hwanghae Province, across the DMZ from Greater Seoul, particularly in the watersheds of the Imjin and Sami Rivers, which served as corridors for raccoon dog movement. Yet I cannot establish precisely when or to what extent forest degradation occurred within these watersheds. This gap makes it difficult to connect North Korea’s economic crisis and South Korea’s zoonotic disease crisis in a historical chronology. What if I approached the vast archive of Landsat images of North Korea, accumulated since the 1970s, through the lens of *distant viewing*? I could compare changes in forest cover at the watershed level before and after the North Korean famine of the 1990s and examine their timing in relation to the resurgence of rabies in South Korea.


Yet these new fragments, too, must be produced through interpretation. As Arnold and Tilton (2023) emphasize, images become data only through the criteria we establish for what to see in them. The figures on North Korean forest degradation that I have relied upon, for example, follow a classification system developed by the South Korean government in preparation for the possible rehabilitation of North Korea’s agricultural infrastructure in the event of a contingency. I therefore hope to develop a different set of criteria for measuring forest degradation, one that takes the ecology of raccoon dogs into account. By showing where my measurements diverge from the existing classifications, I also hope to make visible the interpretive choices that go into assembling my own collage.
