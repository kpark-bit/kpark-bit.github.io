## Annotated Bibliography


**1. Cho, Eunsung 조은성. "근현대 동아시아 '탄소 레짐'의 구축, 팽창, 재편" [The Construction, Expansion, and Reconfiguration of the "Carbon Regime" in Modern East Asia]. Funded project abstract, 2026 Humanities and Social Sciences Research Institute Support Program, National Research Foundation of Korea. Reproduced on the website of the Korean Association for Digital Humanities (KADH), August 13, 2026. https://www.kadh.org/조은성-서강대학교-근현대-동아시아-탄소-레짐의/.**

Cho’s team proposes a long-term digital humanities project that uses historical geographic information systems (HGIS) to reconstruct energy production, circulation, and consumption, the infrastructure that sustained these processes, and its effects on surrounding spaces. I selected this proposal because I am developing a research project on how oil moving along the Trans-Korea Pipeline (TKP) reshaped everyday life in Seoul. The connection is temporal as well as methodological. Whereas Cho’s team focuses on coal from the late nineteenth to the mid-twentieth century, my project takes up the subsequent turn toward oil, mediated by the TKP that United States Forces Korea completed in 1970. I am also interested in the proposal’s regional scope: it approaches the coal regime across East Asia, with imperial Japan as a central node. Following the project’s development could help me consider how to conceive a collaborative history of the oil regime across regions connected by the American military presence and U.S. aid.



**2. Kyŏngsŏng Digital Literary Map Research Team 경성 디지털 문학지도 공동연구팀. *DEEP INTO SEOUL PROJECT*. Project description posted by Kim Byungjun 김병준 on the website of the Korean Association for Digital Humanities (KADH), January 19, 2026. https://www.kadh.org/deep-into-seoul-project-플랫폼/.**

*Deep into Seoul* overlays maps of colonial Seoul (1910–45) with maps of the present-day city, reconstructing places and characters’ movements from early twentieth-century literary texts through maps and networks. For my research on Seoul in the latter half of the twentieth century, when rapid urban expansion repeatedly altered administrative boundaries and road networks, its method of locating historical places against present-day coordinates offers a useful precedent. I was especially interested in its estimates of travel time based on historical transport networks. Last year, I studied an informal system of organic circulation in which food waste from Seoul was carried to pig farms on the urban outskirts and consumed as feed. This system long played a central role in the city’s waste disposal. By the late 1980s, however, traffic congestion had lengthened journeys between urban collection points, raising labor costs and contributing to the system’s rapid decline. Modeling the locations of pig farms and collection sites alongside historical road networks and estimated travel times could help me identify the threshold at which collection ceased to be economically viable. It could also allow me to reconstruct which parts of Seoul fell out of this circulation network first.



**3. Oh, Yoonjong 오윤중. *Global Oil Flow Map by Historical Event*. Student project, KAIST Digital History Exhibition, Spring 2026, https://yoonjongoh.github.io/Digital-History-Project-2026/.**

Oh’s project maps global oil transport routes and examines the relations of power through which these flows are controlled or concealed. What interested me was its attention to spatial patterns across large datasets, extending beyond individual events or places, and its concise presentation of the analysis and findings on a GitHub-based website. I would like to apply a similar approach to my research on rabies control in Greater Seoul. In that work, I argue that North Korea’s food and energy crisis in the 1990s intensified forest degradation, displacing raccoon dogs from their habitats and encouraging their movement southward across the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ). This movement, I argue, contributed to the reemergence of rabies in South Korean border regions. Bringing together spatial data on North Korean forest change, the locations and dates of rabies outbreaks in the South, the likely river corridors of raccoon dog movement, and Greater Seoul’s northward expansion could make the causal relationships I propose easier to follow than prose alone. The project also offers a model for presenting sources, research methods, and visualizations together on the web, making my work accessible to readers beyond academia.



**4. Yoon, Eunsook 윤은숙. "소멸 위기 폐광지역의 산업 유산 아카이빙: GIS 기반 한국 근현대 탄광촌 문헌·구술 통합 DB 구축" [Archiving the Industrial Heritage of Disappearing Coal-Mining Regions: Building a GIS-Based Integrated Database of Documentary and Oral Sources on Modern Korean Mining Communities]. Funded project abstract, 2026 Humanities and Social Sciences Research Institute Support Program, National Research Foundation of Korea. Reproduced on the website of the Korean Association for Digital Humanities (KADH), August 13, 2026. https://www.kadh.org/윤은숙-강원대학교-삼척캠퍼스-소멸-위기-폐광지역/.**

Yoon’s team takes the end of domestic coal mining in twenty-first-century South Korea as the starting point for a digital archive of mining communities. The proposal treats mine closure as a critical juncture at which industrial remains and community memories risk disappearing together. In response, it proposes an archive combining documentary sources, oral histories, and GIS data. I was particularly drawn to its plan to align historical maps with present-day topography, linking records and recollections associated with particular places within a shared spatial framework. This concern with preserving both material traces and memory speaks directly to my planned research on the TKP. Completed in 1970, the pipeline carried oil to the capital region for decades before being dismantled in the early 2000s, yet it has not been the subject of sustained historical research or a dedicated archival project. Reconstructing its history will therefore require attention both to the traces of largely vanished facilities and surrounding landscapes and to the memories of those involved in its construction and operation. My immediate aim is to write a history of the TKP. As I gather sources, however, I also want to keep open the possibility of eventually expanding this work into a digital archival project with support from the South Korean government. Yoon’s proposal helps me think through how such an archive might preserve the material and remembered histories of dismantled energy infrastructure through their connections to place.



**5. Tsing, Anna L., Jennifer Deger, Alder Keleman Saxena, and Feifei Zhou, eds. *Feral Atlas: The More-Than-Human Anthropocene*. Stanford University Press, 2021. https://feralatlas.org/index.html.**

*Feral Atlas* explores the Anthropocene through the unexpected “feral effects” that emerge when industrial and technological infrastructures interact with nonhuman beings beyond their designers’ intentions and control. This concept is particularly useful for interpreting the history of rabies control in Greater Seoul. Through the intensive removal of free-roaming dogs in the 1970s, Seoul brought a principal urban host of the disease under control and subsequently kept rabies largely in check for nearly two decades. Yet this success rested on an implicit assumption that the city and its surroundings could remain ecologically separate. In the 1990s, North Korea’s food and energy crisis and forest degradation, together with development in South Korean border regions, encouraged raccoon dog movement and incursions into human settlements. Rabies reemerged through ecological connections that the existing control regime could not contain. *Feral Atlas* helps me bring South Korean disease control and North Korean economic crisis into a shared history. Its approach directs my attention to how control systems and nonhuman actors interacted in unanticipated ways to produce a new disease ecology.



**6. Robichaud, Andrew, and Erik Steiner. "Trail of Blood: The Movement of San Francisco's Butchertown and the Spatial Transformation of Meat Production, 1849–1901." Working paper, Spatial History Project, Center for Spatial and Textual Analysis, Stanford University, submitted April 1, 2010. https://web.stanford.edu/group/spatialhistory/static/publications/pub31.html.**

“Trail of Blood” uses GIS to reconstruct the movement of slaughterhouses and the meat trade from central San Francisco to the peripheral district of Butchertown in the late nineteenth century. I was drawn to its account of how the spatial separation of animals and slaughter from urban life reorganized entire networks of meat production and distribution. My research follows the informal circulation of food waste from Seoul to pig farms, where it was consumed as feed. These farms did not firmly occupy the urban periphery. As the center of Seoul expanded, they were repeatedly pushed farther outward and eventually beyond its administrative boundaries. Urban expansion thus changed the distances and costs of collecting food waste, affecting the viability of the circulation network as a whole. By combining the locations of slaughterhouses and butcher shops with historical maps, “Trail of Blood” offers a method for tracing comparable changes in Seoul: the repeated displacement of pig farms and the lengthening and reorganization of the routes that connected them to urban food waste.



**7. Booker, Matthew. *Between the Tides*. Spatial History Project, Center for Spatial and Textual Analysis, Stanford University. Accessed September 15, 2026. https://web.stanford.edu/group/spatialhistory/static/projects/project1005.html.**

*Between the Tides* approaches San Francisco Bay as a historical space in which the city and the natural environment have continually reshaped one another. By layering sources on land use, industrial production, ecological change, and waste disposal, it makes their relationships and transformations visible. The project offers another way to think about Greater Seoul, the spatial focus of my research. Its boundaries and functions have been continually remade through the movements and actions of materials and living beings: food waste and pigs, wildlife and disease, oil and pipelines. Understood in these terms, Greater Seoul exceeds both a fixed administrative territory and a passive setting for urbanization. Overlaying these different environmental processes and material flows within the same geographical space could help me ask how they became interconnected and how they operated together as the metropolis’s urban metabolism.



**8. Ryu, Intae 류인태. "디지털 인문학은 인문학이다" [Digital Humanities for Humanities]. *인문논총* [Journal of Humanities] 77, no. 3 (2020): 365–407. https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/landing/article.kci?arti_id=ART002624171.**

Ryu critiques a tendency in Korean digital humanities in which the technical work of building DH projects is outsourced to outside firms, weakening humanities scholars’ intellectual control over the research process. His argument is that humanists themselves need a deeper understanding of how DH projects are designed and built. As someone who hopes eventually to return to Korea and continue my career there, I found the essay especially useful for understanding how scholars in Korea have debated the meaning and direction of digital humanities.
I am not fully persuaded, however, by Ryu’s suggestion that securing intellectual control requires individual humanists to acquire a broad range of technical skills themselves. I am not convinced that humanists need to retain exclusive control over the research process in the first place. Ryu refers to those responsible for the technical work as outside “firms,” but I am inclined to think of these collaborators as scholars. If humanists can learn technical methods, why should those we call “technologists” not also participate in humanistic thinking and interpretation? For me, then, the more productive question is not how humanists can prevent the loss of research authority, but how humanists and technically trained researchers might collaborate from the stage of formulating research questions—and how scholarly credit might be distributed more equitably in the process. 



**9. Kim, Jingyu 김진규. "1910~20년대 한반도 주둔 일본군의 위생관리와 국경 검역활동: 역사지리정보시스템(HGIS) 작업을 중심으로" [Sanitary Management and Border Quarantine Activities of Japanese Garrison Forces in Korea in the 1910s–1920s: Focusing on Historical Geographic Information Systems]. Funded project abstract, 2026 Humanities and Social Sciences Research Professor Program (Type B), National Research Foundation of Korea. Reproduced on the website of the Korean Association for Digital Humanities (KADH), May 14, 2026. https://www.kadh.org/김진규-서울대학교-191020년대-한반도-주둔-일본군의-위/.**

Kim proposes to use HGIS to reconstruct how sanitation and border quarantine operated spatially among Japanese forces stationed in Korea during the 1910s and 1920s. I was struck by the specificity of his research design: historical topographic maps would be overlaid with present-day maps, barracks and detached posts represented as points, connections and movements as lines, and jurisdictions and quarantine zones as polygons and buffers. This design offers a methodological precedent for my research on rabies control in Greater Seoul, particularly the distribution of oral vaccine baits beginning in 2001. My analysis turns on the spatial relationships among outbreak sites, raccoon dog habitats and movement routes, administrative boundaries separating Seoul from its satellite cities, and areas where vaccine baits were distributed. In Seoul, a dense defensive line of vaccine baits was established along the city’s administrative boundary, while surrounding areas received more selective coverage. Mapping these relationships together in HGIS could help me examine both the spread of disease and the uneven geography of protection produced by efforts to control it.



**10. Nelson, Robert K., LaDale Winling, et al. "Mapping Inequality: Redlining in New Deal America." Edited by Robert K. Nelson. *American Panorama: An Atlas of United States History*, 2023. https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining.**

*Mapping Inequality* reconstructs as spatial data the residential security maps and neighborhood assessments produced by U.S. federal housing agencies in the 1930s. In doing so, the project makes visible how racial and social discrimination became institutionalized across urban space. More importantly, it makes it easier to ask how the effects of policy varied between neighborhoods within the same city.

The approach to visualize disparity is especially suggestive for my own project. The benefits and burdens generated through processes of urban metabolism were certainly not distributed evenly across the metropolis, yet I have found it difficult to identify patterns that appear only intermittently and unevenly across the urban landscape. Mapping Inequality helped me see visualization not simply as a way to present conclusions, but as a tool for generating historical questions. Mapping differences among the districts and neighborhoods of Greater Seoul could make spatial disparities visible at a glance, allowing me both to test questions I already have and to notice patterns that might otherwise remain difficult to see.



**How I Found These Sources**

I gathered these DH sources by following connections among scholars and institutions I encountered through coursework and events. I first learned about Cho’s research on East Asian carbon regimes after meeting him at an international conference on contemporary Korean studies this summer. I encountered *Deep into Seoul* through Professor Kwon Eun’s presentation at a summer workshop at Hanyang University. After a course reading introduced me to Stanford’s substantial investment in digital humanities, I explored its project archive and found “Trail of Blood” and *Between the Tides*. Dr. Jennifer Marine’s virtual visit to our class prompted another search: learning of her affiliation with the University of Richmond led me to explore the university’s digital humanities projects, where I found *Mapping Inequality*.

Alongside these connections, I conducted keyword searches guided by my research interests. To learn how digital humanities has been understood in South Korea, I searched Google Scholar for “디지털 인문학 (digital humanities)” and found Ryu Intae’s article. A Google search for Korean digital history projects led me to KAIST’s Digital History Exhibition. While looking for digital humanities projects in environmental history, I reached McGill University’s Digital Environmental Humanities page and followed its project list to *Feral Atlas*. Exploring the projects listed by the Korean Association for Digital Humanities also led me to the proposals by Yoon Eunsook and Kim Jingyu. Throughout this process, my interests in HGIS and spatial analysis, environmental history, infrastructure and material flows, and disease and nonhuman actors guided both the search and the selection.
