Data Feminism (Ken Park)  

Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein. Data Feminism, 2020. 

"Introduction: Why Data Science Needs Feminism" 

Summary of the reading 

Data are not simply collections of objective facts. How they are produced, analyzed, and used is entangled with power structures. Yet data are not inherently oppressive. While they have long been used to reproduce existing structures of power, they can also become tools to challenge them. 

Social justice is at the center of data feminism. It examines how conventional practices in data science reproduce existing inequalities and at the same time it seeks to use data to challenge and transform unequal power relations. 

The justice that data feminism envisions extends beyond reconfirming the inequalities faced by marginalized groups. By producing more inclusive and reliable knowledge, it seeks to provide a foundation for society as a whole to challenge oppressive power structures reinforced by current data-related practices. 


Discussion Questions 

What counts as data? 

What do data reveal, and what do they conceal? 

When does data collection become an instrument of power? 

What makes data science feminist? 

How might a data feminist perspective inform research in your field? 

 

"Our Values and Our Metrics for Holding Ourselves Accountable" 

Summary of the reading 

The appendix not only asks 'What values do we advocate?' but also 'How much does the knowledge we produce embody those values?'  

Turning their own work into an object of analysis, the authors use quantitative metrics to assess power relations within the academic world they inhabit.  

The authors share a concern that, without critically reflecting on their own research in public, they might unwittingly reproduce the very power relations they seek to challenge.  

The appendix presents the authors' stated values, their personal and academic backgrounds, the metrics they use, and their reflections on what those metrics reveal. 


Discussion Questions 

How well do the authors' metrics reflect their five core values? 

What do the authors' metrics reveal, and what do they leave out? 

What does this case reveal about academic peer review? 

How might you apply these values and metrics to your own research?

Summary of the reading 

Lauren Klein, "Between Data and Truth: W.E.B. Du Bois's 'Data Portraits" 

Klein looks at the charts Du Bois and his students made for the 1900 Paris Exposition, showing how they used data to challenge racist ideas about Black Americans.  

The charts highlighted changes in education, employment, and property ownership after emancipation. Their colors and unusual designs helped tell this story. 

Photographs appeared alongside the charts, giving visitors a view of individual lives that statistics alone could not provide.  


Summary of the reading 

Aiyegbusi, '' Decolonizing Digital Humanities: Africa in Perspective'' 

Aiyegbusi asks why DH is often seen as a Western field and what this means for scholars working in Africa, particularly Nigeria. 

Expensive internet access, unreliable electricity, and limited institutional support make participation difficult. Even an unpaid online task can cost participants money. 

DH`s unclear boundaries and the limited visibility of African projects can also leave scholars unsure of where their work fits. 

Including more African materials in digital projects in only part of the task. Local scholars also need opportunities to develop projects and shape the field.  

 

Afanador- Llach and Lombana- Bermudez, '' Developing New Literacy Skills and Digital Scholarship Infrastructures in the Global South: A case study'' 

Summary of the reading  

This chapter follows a volunteer project to catalog colonial documents in Colombia. Although the documents had been digitated, researchers still needed a catalog to find and use them.  

Without the resources to build a dedicated platform, the team used Google applications and Facebook to organize the work and share questions.  

Volunteers learned paleography and solved problems together. The authors describe this learning through the concepts of collective intelligence and distributed cognition.  

Keeping people involved proved difficult. The work required considerable time and expertise, showing how a project's low financial cost can depend on a great deal of unpaid labor.  

 

Discussion Questions 

Turn to a partner and consider: 

How does the Colombian project change our understanding of what counts as DH infrastructure? 

When does volunteer collaboration challenge inequality, and when does it reproduce it? 

Does '' makeshift'' infrastructure reduce cost, or shift them onto participants? 

How might defining DH more clearly both enable and restrict participation? 

What distinguishes expanding DH globally from decolonizing it? 

We discussed '' yacking' and hacking earlier in the semester.  How do electricity and internet access affect these activities in Aiyegbusi`s account? 

How does Du Bois`s use of photographs alongside charts change what the exhibition allows us to understand? 

When does demanding more data about injustice help challenge it, and when might it delay action ? 

How does recovering the students contributions change our understanding of Du Bois`s data portraits?

https://stoplapdspyingarchive.org/   


3. The Pudding — Film Dialogue from 2,000 Screenplays, Broken Down by Gender and Age 

http://pudding.cool/2017/03/film-dialogue/  


4. The Brownies' Book: Legacies of Black Childhood 

A digital exhibition on an African American children's magazine from the 1920s. 

https://thebrowniesbook.omeka.net/  


5. South Asian American Digital Archive (SAADA) — Exhibits 

A digital archive documenting the histories and identities of South Asian American communities. 

https://www.saada.org/explore/exhibits  


6. Anti-Eviction Mapping Project 

A project using maps and oral histories to document the impact of evictions and gentrification on local communities. 

https://antievictionmappingproject.wordpress.com/ 


7. Colored Conventions Project  

A digital exhibition on Black Americans' struggles for rights in the nineteenth century. 

https://coloredconventions.org/ 


8. Scribes of the Cairo Geniza  

A crowdsourcing project in which volunteers help classify and transcribe documents from medieval Jewish communities. 

https://www.scribesofthecairogeniza.org/ 


9. South African History Online (SAHO) — Timelines  

An online resource on South African history and culture. 

https://sahistory.org.za/timelines

A collaborative project with Indigenous communities that sheds light on the history of Indigenous enslavement and forced labor in America. 

https://stolenrelations.org/tribal-community-partners-information-and-links/
